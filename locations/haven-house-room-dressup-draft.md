# 145 Berkeley Place — Haven House
## Spatial and Interior Reference — Working Draft

This file is being rebuilt floor by floor from the original architectural plan and Alice's annotated dress-up copies. It is a working source for the eventual canonical room description and SillyTavern Lorebook. The marked plans govern walls, doors, stairs, fixed utilities, and circulation; the prose below records furnishing, use, sightlines, atmosphere, and the way the household inhabits each space.

---

## Cellar

### Topology and circulation

The cellar is a single long, narrow level rather than a corridor with several rooms branching from it. The main staircase arrives a little below the midpoint along the right-hand wall. From the foot of the stairs, the open floor extends in both directions: the gym occupies the broad lower end of the cellar, while the upper end narrows into storage before terminating at the wine racks. Theo's safe room is the only fully enclosed room on the level, tucked into the upper-left side and entered directly from the shared cellar space.

The right-hand wall provides the cellar's practical spine. Weapons and training equipment are stored along its lower run, beginning beyond the stairs and continuing through the gym area. Farther toward the upper end, this gives way to deep shelves used for general household storage. Gas, water, and electrical services remain exposed at the lower end of the cellar; nothing is positioned so that it obstructs access to them.

Standing at the foot of the stairs, a person can see most of the gym and the long equipment wall immediately. The safe-room door is farther away and partly screened by the change in width and the storage arranged around it. There is no direct sightline into the safe room from the staircase, even when its door is open.

### Gym and training area

The gym claims the widest, most open part of the cellar. Rubber matting covers the old floor through the principal training zone, cushioning falls without attempting to disguise the age of the building beneath it. A weight bench and free weights occupy one side; Jackie's heavy bag hangs where it has enough clearance to swing. The centre stays deliberately open for bodyweight work, sparring, and wand practice rather than being divided into a separate duelling room.

Weapons and equipment run along the right-hand wall: practice blades, staffs, protective gear, target discs, and the less elegant miscellany accumulated by three people who train for very different kinds of danger. Everything dangerous has a fixed place, though the system is practical rather than decorative. Frequently used pieces remain within easy reach of the mats; specialist or spare equipment sits higher or farther back.

The room is functional and unceremonious—cool stone, rubber, leather, metal, and the faint mineral damp of an old London cellar under temperature and moisture charms. There are no ornamental displays and no need to make the work look graceful. Sound travels the full length of the level: impacts against the heavy bag, dropped weights, spellfire, or a body hitting the mat can be heard from the storage end and inside Theo's safe room unless its door is shut and the room's silencing wards are engaged.

### General storage and wine

Beyond the staircase, the cellar shifts gradually from training space into storage rather than crossing a formal threshold. Tall shelving follows the upper-right wall, holding reserve household supplies, preserved ingredients, repair materials, and the boxes that have no reason to live upstairs but cannot yet be discarded. Opposite it, immediately outside Theo's safe room, is another compact area of general storage.

A single old barrel sits among those supplies. It belonged to the cellar before its current arrangement and has never acquired a settled explanation: it may contain wine, something magical, or merely the former owner's commitment to atmosphere. Until opened on-page, its contents remain unspecified.

Wine racks occupy the recess at the far upper-left end of the plan, beyond the safe-room door. They are extensive enough to be part of the cellar's architecture rather than a small freestanding rack. Bottles are kept horizontally in the naturally cooler end of the level. Draco maintains enough order that a particular vintage can be found without excavation; Jackie is more likely to remember a bottle by the occasion for which she bought it.

### Theo's safe room

Theo's safe room is a compact rectangular room built into the upper-left side of the cellar. Its only door opens into the storage end of the main cellar. It has no windows and no route deeper into the house. The walls are stone, reinforced by lightproofing, preservation, silencing, and containment wards designed for privacy and safety without turning the room into a cage.

A narrow cot runs along the upper wall. A small desk and chair fit against the right side, giving Theo somewhere to read, write, or wait through the hours when lying still becomes intolerable. A mini-fridge stands near the door and holds emergency blood bags under redundant cooling and preservation charms. The furnishings leave a clear path between cot, desk, refrigerator, and exit; even at its most crowded, the room does not force him to negotiate obstacles while hungry or disoriented.

It is spare because its purpose is narrow, not because no one cared enough to furnish it. Jackie built it from Filippo's description of the quiet room in Florence: somewhere Theo could retreat when the hunger or the Malice became too loud, and somewhere the others could bring blood without exposing the rest of the house. But Haven House's version makes one principle explicit. The door can be secured against interruption and the wards can contain violence in an emergency, but Theo is not stored here. There are no bars, no viewing window, and nothing in the room is meant to punish him for needing it.

With the door open, the safe room looks out only onto stored supplies and the opposite shelving—not the gym, the stairs, or the household moving overhead. With it closed, the level falls away. The refrigerator's low hum and the small pool of working light over the desk are often the only evidence that the room belongs to a lived-in house rather than the foundations beneath one.

## Garden floor

### Topology and circulation

The garden floor runs from the street-level entrance at the front of the house to the rear garden. The front door opens through a small entrance vestibule directly into the office/study, which occupies the broad street-facing front half of the floor. The circulation route is not a separate enclosed hall here: it follows the office's right-hand side toward the central stairwell and continues past the bathroom and storage block to the herbary door at the rear. The herbary fills the large garden-facing room beyond that central service zone.

The office therefore has no internal door separating it from the entrance or circulation path: anyone coming in from the street arrives in the working room itself. Farther back, a distinct door beside the stairwell enters the herbary near its front-right corner. The bathroom opens from the passage opposite the stairs, while the adjoining storage cupboard opens toward the office. The herbary's rear wall is almost entirely glass, with a sliding door opening directly into the garden.

This makes the floor a practical working route through the house. Visitors arriving by the front door enter the office without crossing a domestic room; those arriving through its fireplace materialise in the same controlled workspace close to the street exit. The bathroom and storage occupy the middle of the plan but are not rooms one passes through: both sit off the circulation route, and the herbary has its own door beyond them. Plants, soil, and potions supplies can move directly between herbary and garden without being carried upstairs.

### Office and study

The office is the principal shared workroom for Jackie's Auror and investigative work and Draco's Ministry work. It is a long, street-facing room with a bay window at the front and a fireplace set into the left wall closer to the rear. The fireplace is connected to the Floo Network. Jackie and Draco use it to travel to the Ministry, and it provides a controlled arrival point for visitors who come by Floo rather than through the front door.

The long main desk sits along the left side of the room and is divided cleanly by habit rather than by a physical partition. Its left half belongs to Jackie: parchment in active stacks, dried quills she has not yet thrown away, manila case folders, notes laid across other notes, and small seized artefacts waiting to be identified, documented, or transferred into evidence storage. The disorder is legible to her even when it looks archaeological to anyone else. Draco's right half is its perfect contrast. An inkwell and several expensive quills are arranged precisely; a dedicated shelf holds folders and documentation; the working surface itself is kept nearly empty. Neither half gradually colonises the other. The boundary has become too obvious to require discussion.

A second table stands against the right side of the room for meetings and the material that needs to be spread out between several people without invading either half of the main desk. It can take open files, maps, case photographs, and multiple chairs, making the office usable by the Liverpool team or Ministry visitors as well as by Jackie and Draco alone. The central floor remains clear enough to move between the hall door, meeting table, desks, fireplace, and bay window without navigating piles.

The bay gives the room its best natural light. In the shallow front recess, a small table and wrought-iron chair provide somewhere separate from the working desks. Jackie uses it when the office has become frustrating and she needs to sit near the window without pretending she has stopped thinking about the case. From there she can look toward the street while remaining in the room with Draco.

The office is orderly at the scale of the room and divided at the scale of personality. Draco's systems keep formal records findable; Jackie's active clutter records the actual motion of an investigation. The result is not a compromise in which both become tidier or messier. It is a shared room that permits them to work differently beside one another.

### Herbary

The herbary occupies the large rear room and feels less like a conventional greenhouse than an inside jungle held within the bones of the house. Floor-to-ceiling windows span the garden-facing wall. Black iron muntins form a square lattice across the glass, dark geometry almost swallowed by leaves, and a sliding glazed door opens straight into the garden. The room has the brightest unfiltered daylight in Haven House.

It is the only room that has not been treated with the house's sun-repelling spell. The spell blocks ultraviolet light completely; excellent for Theo, fatal to the purpose of a growing room. Daylight therefore remains physically dangerous to him here. During the day, the herbary is not part of his safe route through the house, and the closed hall door and solid front section of wall prevent its light from spilling freely into the corridor. At night, the restriction disappears and the room returns to the household's shared territory.

Plants occupy every available height. Large pots stand directly on the floor; smaller pots cluster around them and hang overhead. Two long growing tables sit lengthwise through the rear half of the room, each covered with different species arranged in formations that appear random until Jackie needs to find something. Additional plants gather through the open central area in mismatched pots, leaving irregular walking paths rather than formal aisles. The placement changes as specimens are harvested, quarantined, repotted, or moved toward better light.

The dense growth alters every sightline. From the hall door, a person sees leaves, hanging vines, and portions of the central worktables before they see the rear glass. From the garden, the house beyond is filtered through the iron lattice and layers of green. Conversation is softened by soil, foliage, and the close humid air, though the clink of glass or metal at the potions station still carries clearly across the room.

The air smells of damp earth, crushed leaves, resin, and whatever has most recently been cut or brewed. Temperature and humidity charms keep the plants stable without sterilising the room into a laboratory. Water, loose soil, pruning tools, labels, and propagation jars are part of its ordinary working state. It is abundant rather than decorative: beautiful because it is alive and used, not because anybody has forced it into symmetry.

### Potions station

The potions station is built into the front-left side of the herbary, immediately behind the bathroom wall. An L-shaped work surface follows the wall, leaving the centre of the herbary open. Several cauldrons occupy the heatproofed section; shelves and cupboards above and below hold ingredients, tools, bottles, and works in progress. Its position keeps brewing close to the living plants and garden supply route while giving flame, fumes, and volatile ingredients a fixed working zone.

Unlike the office desk, the station has no permanent Jackie side and Draco side. Its current appearance reveals who has used it most recently. Under Draco's rule, ingredients return to labelled positions, tools align, surfaces clear, and the next stage of a brew waits with exact notes. Under Jackie's, active ingredients accumulate around the relevant cauldron, implements remain where her hand last needed them, and several lines of work may coexist in a system that looks accidental from the outside. The territory shifts back and forth rather than meeting at a stable border.

This makes the station a constant, low-grade battle between Draco's order and Jackie's chaos, but a productive one. Draco can reconstruct and standardise; Jackie can improvise and follow a problem sideways. Each complains about the evidence left by the other and each knows how to read it. A newly immaculate station or a sudden bloom of clutter is often enough to tell the household who has been working there without asking.

### Bathroom, storage, and garden access

The garden-floor bathroom is a compact full bathroom opening from the hall between office and herbary. It contains a bath-shower, toilet, and basin, useful for anyone working with soil, potion residue, or seized material without requiring them to carry the mess through the rest of the house. A shallow double cupboard occupies the corresponding recess on the office side of the service block and holds office supplies, evidence-safe containers, and practical overflow that does not belong on the visible shelves.

The herbary's sliding door is the floor's direct route into the rear garden. Immediately outside is the paved lower patio and the foot of the exterior spiral staircase descending from the parlour-floor deck. Beyond it, the garden extends the full narrow length of the plot. This creates two distinct exterior approaches: the formal street entrance into the hall and office, and the private garden approach into the herbary. Someone on the deck above can see portions of the patio and garden, but foliage and the depth of the plot interrupt a complete view into the herbary itself.

The transition is deliberately porous. Pots can migrate outside in suitable weather; cuttings and harvested ingredients travel only a few steps to the potions station; soil on the threshold is an ordinary consequence rather than a housekeeping failure. The garden-facing end of the floor belongs to growth, brewing, and the untidy material reality of Jackie's work. The street-facing end belongs to records, interviews, and the version of that work that must survive Ministry scrutiny. The corridor joins them without confusing one for the other.

## Parlour floor

### Topology and circulation

The parlour floor is the principal domestic and social level of Haven House. The front door opens into a small foyer at the street-facing end. The dining room occupies the broad front room beside it, while a long side corridor runs past the dining-room wall toward the central staircase and the rooms at the rear. A coat rack stands near the foyer partition; opposite it, a narrow side table and mirror make a practical place for keys, gloves, and the last check before leaving the house.

The kitchen occupies the narrow middle section beyond the stairs and opens from the corridor. It is not a passage between the dining room and living room: each room has its own entrance from the circulation route. The living room fills the garden-facing rear of the floor and opens through glazed double doors onto the deck. From the deck, an exterior spiral staircase descends to the lower patio and garden-floor herbary entrance.

This arrangement keeps formal arrivals at the front and the household's most relaxed territory at the rear. A visitor can be taken directly into the dining room without crossing the kitchen or living room; someone moving through the house can pass the dining room, stairs, and kitchen before entering the living room or stepping outside. The long corridor and central stairwell carry voices between rooms more readily than the closed walls do, while the rear doors bring garden sounds into the living room when open.

### Dining room

The dining room occupies the street-facing front of the floor, with a broad bay window holding the room's large oval table. The table is sized for the household and guests rather than intimate meals alone. Its position gives everyone seated around it the best of the front light and leaves space to circulate between the bay, the cabinet, and the doorway from the foyer.

A display cabinet holds fine china brought from Nott Manor. The pieces are expensive, old, and almost never used. Their presence is less about practical dining than continuity: an inheritance removed from the Manor and made part of Haven House without requiring the new household to reproduce the old one's rituals. Behind glass, the china remains immaculate and visible—claimed, preserved, and largely spared the indignity of actual dinner.

The room is the most formal on the floor, but not a museum. The table can carry ordinary meals, paperwork displaced from downstairs, or enough food and wine for friends who have stayed past the point of ceremony. The cabinet gives the room inherited grandeur; the coat rack and foyer just beyond its door keep it attached to daily life.

### Kitchen

The kitchen is a compact galley in the middle of the floor, entered from the side corridor. Cupboards and work surfaces run along both walls, keeping the centre clear as a narrow working aisle. Along the principal appliance run, the refrigerator stands toward the front; farther along are the sink and the stove immediately beside it. Storage above and below keeps cookware, ingredients, and the less glamorous machinery of feeding three people within reach.

A small table fits at the rear end of the room. It is better suited to coffee, a hurried breakfast, or one person keeping another company while they cook than to a formal meal; the dining room is only a short walk away when the household needs space. Because the kitchen does not connect directly through to either neighbouring room, cooking smells and conversation escape chiefly into the corridor when the door is open rather than turning the kitchen into a route everyone must cross.

The room is practical rather than showy. It can support Draco's precision, Jackie's improvisation, and Theo's presence without pretending they approach food or domestic work in the same way. Its scale makes avoidance difficult when two people are working at once: they must negotiate the same aisle, the same counters, and the stove beside the sink.

### Living room

The living room occupies the full garden-facing rear of the floor. A decorative fireplace stands along the left wall; unlike the office fireplace below, it is not connected to the Floo Network. Bookshelves continue along the lower-left section, keeping frequently read and casually abandoned books in the room where people actually settle with them.

A velvet sofa runs along the right side, facing inward toward a central coffee table and the fireplace side of the room. Two separate armchairs complete the seating group. Theo's stands nearer the kitchen-side entrance; Jackie's sits closer to the deck doors. Their placement creates distinct habitual territories without turning the room into three isolated stations: anyone in either chair can speak easily to the sofa, see the centre of the room, and remain part of whatever is happening there.

Built-in storage occupies part of the right wall. Near the glazed doors to the deck, a low cabinet serves both as a side table and as the household's liquor cabinet. Bottles and glasses are kept inside rather than displayed across the room, close enough to the seating and deck to make pouring a drink easy without making alcohol the room's visual centre.

The room is Haven House at its least formal. The dining room faces visitors and inheritance; the living room faces the garden and the people who already belong. From the sofa and chairs, the rear glass brings in the deck, weather, and movement outside. With the doors open, conversation can spill onto the deck; with them closed, the room becomes a contained refuge at the farthest point from the street entrance.

### Deck

Glazed double doors open from the living room onto the raised rear deck. A table and chairs make it an outdoor extension of the living space rather than merely a landing. It looks over the narrow rear garden and the lower patio outside the herbary, with the iron spiral staircase descending along one side to connect the parlour and garden floors without requiring anyone to cross the interior stairs.

The deck is therefore both a destination and a route. Drinks can come directly from the liquor cabinet beside the doors; meals can be carried from the kitchen along the corridor and through the living room; someone working in the garden or herbary can come up by the spiral stair. Voices on the deck carry down toward the patio and garden more easily than into the street-facing rooms, while the living room remains immediately visible through the glass.
