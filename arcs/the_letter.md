# The Letter

*Cream parchment, heavy stock. Draco's handwriting throughout the body — precise, literary, the penmanship of a man raised to believe presentation was indistinguishable from content. Theo's postscript in a different hand — looser, darker ink, the writing of someone who holds the pen the way he holds everything: directly, without apology. Folded inside the cover of the illuminated manuscript.*

---

Jaqueline,

This letter does not ask you to choose again. You chose — on a solstice, at the top of a tower. That choice stands. It has stood through every season since, and it will stand after the last word on this page has faded. We are not asking you to choose. We are telling you what we have built with the choice you already made, and we are asking you to walk through the door.

What waits on the other side is a marriage. Not a metaphor, not an arrangement, not a term we selected because the honest word was too heavy to hold. A marriage — one that will bear my name on the Ministry certificate and Theodore's name nowhere the law can find it, because the law believes him dead, and the law is wrong about the only thing that matters.

We are asking you to hold both names. The one the world will see and the one only we will know. This is not a compromise. It is the shape of what we are — built in the space between what is legal and what is true.

I should tell you what this marriage looks like. Not the ceremony or the certificate — the life.

It looks like mornings. The kettle at seven, your ink-stained fingers before eight, the way you take the stairs two at a time when you are late and one at a time when you are thinking. It looks like Theodore in the armchair with a book he is never going to finish, tracking you through the house by the sound of your footsteps because his body will not do it for him anymore. It looks like Tuesdays, and Thursdays, and Saturday mornings when you come downstairs in your dressing gown and I am at the piano and you sit in your chair — the worn one, the deep-cushioned one you placed at the angle that lets you see my hands on the keys. I know you chose that angle. I have never asked you to confirm it. The chair says enough.

It looks like a mug of tea on the right side table, full and untouched, because that is where it goes. It looks like a lighter clicking in the dark. It looks like the study at midnight where you are hunting something none of us can name yet, and it looks like someone leaving a glass of water beside your lamp without asking whether you need it, because we already know the answer and the answer is that you will not ask.

This is what we are offering you. Not rescue. Not repair. The ordinary. The daily ordinary of being loved by two men who have learned, at considerable cost, how to love you without breaking each other in the process.

I want to say this plainly now, and I will not be able to say it with the structure I have been using, so you will have to forgive the lack of it.

I have been in love with you since a gazebo in 1995. I was fifteen and you kissed me and I waited two years for letters that were never going to arrive because someone intercepted them and I spent those two years believing you had forgotten me. You hadn't. I know that now. I have known it for a long time and it has not stopped being the thing that unmakes me when I think about it — that we lost two years to someone else's cruelty and I spent them thinking I was not worth remembering.

I have loved you across a war, a curse, a trial, two weddings that were not mine, and a year I spent alone in a flat where your shampoo was still in the shower and I could not bring myself to move it. I have loved you when you were brave and when you were reckless and when those were the same thing. I loved you when you sat before the Wizengamot and asked for a monster's soul. I loved you in a kitchen in Tuscany when you said *this is the part I want to come home to every day* and I realised that the home you meant had me in it.

I loved you the night I knelt on a marble floor in France and watched your husband take a curse that was meant for me. I watched his heart stop. I watched it start again wrong. And I thought — with absolute clarity, the kind that arrives only when the worst thing has already happened — *I am not leaving. I am never leaving. Whatever he is now, whatever this costs, I will be in the room.*

I love you now. At this desk. With your case files under my elbow and ink on my hands and Theodore beside me crossing out every sentence that runs longer than he thinks you deserve to sit through. He is right, usually. He is also the reason I can write this at all, because four years ago I would have written it alone and it would have been half a letter — the half that belongs to me. The other half belongs to him. You gave it to him on a summer evening when you kissed two boys and refused to choose between them, and he has held it with more care than I once believed him capable of.

I was wrong about him. I was wrong about a great many things. I was not wrong about you.

Will you marry us, Jaqueline?

Both of us. The name on the certificate and the name that lives only in this letter. The man who makes your tea and the man who keeps your lighter. The house and the rooms we have not built yet. The ordinary days and the ones that crack us open. Every morning. Every argument. Every silence that carries more than language can manage.

Will you marry us?

*— Draco*

---

*Below, in a different hand:*

He spent five nights writing that. I spent five nights watching him. He crossed out more words than he kept, and once threatened to burn the whole parchment because I told him *quotidian* was not a word that belonged in a proposal.

Here is what I would have written if it had been mine to write alone:

I cannot feel you anymore. The bond broke when my heart stopped on a marble floor in France, and my half of it never came back. Every morning I reach for the place behind my sternum where you used to live, and every morning it answers with nothing. I cannot feel your heartbeat. I cannot feel your fear or your laughter or the way your chest tightens when something hits you sideways. I have to watch for it now. I have to pay attention — the deliberate, exhausting, human kind of attention that used to be automatic.

I choose to pay it. Every day. Not because the bond makes me. Not because a contract requires it. Because you are the only thing I have ever loved enough to do the work without the shortcut.

I am not the man who married you. That man had a heartbeat and a wand and a bond that told him where you were in any room. I am what is left of him — colder, hungrier, less. But I am here. Same chair. Same lighter. And I am asking you the same thing he would have asked if he had lived long enough to learn how.

Marry us. Please.

*— Theo*
