### Sable

Filippo carried the bags into the dining room without haste. The paper handles had drawn taut beneath their weight, cutting clean lines across his fingers without leaving any colour behind.

He set them on the table one at a time.

Warmth bled through the brown paper into the dark wood. The spices were dense enough for even a human nose—cumin, roasted garlic, cardamom, hot oil—but to Filippo they separated into precise layers. Chicken in one carton. Lamb in another. Basmati rice, coriander, charred flour. Food remembered by the senses of a body that had not needed it in more than a century.

Behind them, sunlight lay across the entrance hall in a bright, lethal bar. It stopped at the dining-room arch. Filippo had stopped well before it.

“At three-twenty this morning,” he said.

His hands moved over the bags, turning each so that its folded mouth faced the centre of the table. The habit belonged to no practical need Jackie could identify. He simply disliked disorder when order cost nothing.

“Before that—Rome. At the turn of the year.”

He glanced at her. His crimson eyes held hers for a moment, then shifted to the closed curtains.

“You have been present for nearly every journey I have made in the past twelve months. France. Rome. France again. London.” The corner of his mouth moved by barely a degree. “I had not realised you were keeping such an effective travel record, cara.”

The muffling charm upstairs had changed the house. Footsteps and floorboards had vanished; the ceiling now gave them nothing. Below it, the dining room felt unusually enclosed—sun barred from one side, the rest of the household sealed away above, the smell of a meal only one of them could eat gathering between them.

Filippo remained beside the table rather than taking a chair. Stillness settled over him once the bags no longer required his hands.

“There was a period when I left Florence for years at a time,” he continued. “Decades, in fact. I knew doors throughout Europe and possessed keys to none of them. At the time I considered that freedom.”

His gaze went to the gold signet on his left hand. Not quite looking at it. Merely choosing somewhere other than her face.

“Eventually one grows tired of arriving everywhere as a guest.”

The quiet after that sentence lasted long enough to lose any claim to accident.

Then Filippo looked at her again.

“But I do not think you were asking for an entry in my itinerary.” His head tilted slightly. “Are you wondering whether I miss the world—or whether Florence has become another sort of contract?”

Filippo did not answer at once.

Cardboard whispered against cardboard as he opened the first bag and lifted out the cartons Jackie had not yet reached. He arranged them down the centre of the table, reading each small handwritten label before setting it aside. The work required almost none of his attention. Still, he gave it the same unhurried precision he gave everything else.

Porcelain touched wood behind him. A drawer opened; cutlery shifted with a muted silver clatter.

At the entrance to the room, the sunlight had moved another inch across the floor.

“It is troublesome,” he said at last. “It is rarely impossible.”

He unfolded the top of a rice carton. Steam rose into his face, rich with saffron and butter. His body recognised every ingredient. Hunger did not follow.

“Portkeys do not object to the hour. Nor do tunnels, covered carriages, warded houses, or sufficiently well-paid hotel managers. One travels after sunset. One confirms the destination before departure. One does not trust curtains fitted by strangers.” His eyes flicked towards the bright hall. “And one learns very quickly which companions consider punctuality a moral virtue.”

The answer was dry, but it did not dismiss her words.

Filippo’s fingers rested against the warm cardboard. For a moment his attention seemed to go through it, elsewhere.

He remembered sunlight.

Not as pain. As heat caught in pale stone. Dust turning gold above an open book. Wisteria throwing narrow shadows across his mother’s loggia. The uncomplicated pleasure of closing his eyes and lifting his face towards noon.

Caterina had offered him forever. She had neglected to itemise it.

“The greater difficulty is not movement,” he said. “It is arrival.”

He raised his gaze to Jackie.

“A city in darkness is not the same city. Markets are gone. Gardens lose their colour. Museums close, unless one has the influence to have them opened—and once a place has been emptied especially for you, it ceases to be ordinary. It becomes a performance.”

His hand left the carton.

“There are beauties which belong only to night. Streets after rain. Music carried through open windows. Architecture without crowds obscuring it. People are often more honest when they believe the respectable part of the day has ended.” A fractional pause. “I would not call the world I have seen incomplete.”

His eyes moved to the blocked sunlight again.

“But there are mornings I remember.”

The sentence came without ornament. He let it remain where it fell.

Then the quality of his attention changed. It returned to her fully—not the steady assessment he gave strangers, but the quieter focus Jackie had learned from his letters, when a question had begun forming long before he allowed it into words.

“You are thinking of Theodore.”

Not accusation. Not even surprise.

Filippo drew out the chair nearest the shaded wall, though he did not sit.

“You are not wondering whether I can cross a continent. You are wondering how the three of you cross one without leaving him behind for half of it.”

His gaze dropped briefly to the place set for a man who could no longer eat, then rose again.

“Where do you want to take him, cara?”

The word failed to cross her lips. Filippo heard it nonetheless.

Steam gathered beneath the clear lids of the cartons and misted them from within. Along the edge of the dining-room arch, the band of sunlight had advanced another finger’s width, bright enough to pull copper from the grain of the floorboards. The rest of the room remained cool and dim. A place arranged around an absence.

Filippo’s gaze settled on the last plate Jackie had put down.

His hand went inside his coat and returned with the old pocket watch. He did not open it. The mechanism had been silent for more than a century; there was nothing on its face he needed to consult. He held it loosely between his fingers, the worn gold chain slipping once across his knuckles before becoming still.

“Dead,” he said.

The word was quiet. It did not strike the table so much as take the empty seat between them.

“You may say it, cara. Death has never required courtesy from me, and there is little dignity in pretending it cannot hear us.”

His thumb passed once over the watch’s case. When he looked at Jackie again, the accustomed trace of amusement was absent from his face.

“You once promised to find Elisabetta, when your own road takes you wherever hers did. I should be offended if you began treating the destination as unspeakable merely because I had entered the room.”

The old gold disappeared back into his coat. His hand remained there for a moment after releasing it, then fell to his side.

“What you want for Theodore is kind. It is also not quite what travel can give him.”

Filippo turned towards the windows. The charm worked so cleanly that the afternoon beyond them appeared untouched: pale sky, neighbouring brick, the high leaves shifting where the breeze reached them. Sunlight filled the glass and stopped there. No scorch. No heat. No danger. A perfect image of a day he could not enter.

“I had crossed most of Europe before Elisabetta died. I knew cities whose names have changed twice since, roads that no longer exist, houses reduced to foundations beneath other houses. None of it made the room less empty when she was gone.” His voice remained level. “A map is poor armour against grief.”

He studied the impossible daylight a little longer.

“But the world continued. I hated it for that. Later, I was grateful.”

The admission stayed plain, stripped of the elegant phrasing he usually placed between himself and anything costly.

“Theodore will not remain part of the world because he has seen enough of it before you die. If he outlives you both, no collection of beautiful places will compensate him. Nor should it.” Filippo’s crimson gaze returned to her. “He will remain because there are still choices that belong to him. Work he has not finished. Doors he wishes to open. People he has permitted to matter. Perhaps an old vampire who continues offering advice long after it has become intolerable.”

A faint shift touched one corner of his mouth and vanished.

“Take him travelling. Show him Paris after midnight and Rome before dawn. Have museums opened, hire drivers who understand why the curtains remain closed, and let him insult every hotel that mistakes blackout fabric for decoration. There are cities far kinder to the night than London, and winters in the north where the sun scarcely troubles the horizon. I know the routes. I know the doors.”

He rested two fingers against the back of the chair he had drawn out, but still did not sit.

“Give him those things because he is here to receive them—not as rehearsals for losing you.”

The house above them remained sealed in its muffled quiet. Beneath it, the food continued to breathe heat into a room where only one person would eat.

“And let the family estate be one journey he cannot make,” Filippo said. “One absence is not abandonment. It is not the first half of forever.”

### Sable

The question reached him across the steam.

Filippo watched Jackie eat. The curry’s heat had coloured her face; saffron clung to the rice, and a curl of fragrant air rose each time her fork disturbed it. Garlic, tomato, coriander, cream. His senses separated every ingredient with perfect accuracy and produced no hunger at all.

He had once loved food from this part of the world. He had forgotten that until now.

“Before Elisabetta?” he said. “No.”

He drew out the chair opposite Jackie and sat at last. The movement was smooth and unhurried, though the answer had altered his stillness. His forearms rested lightly against the table, safely distant from the spread of dishes.

“I was born an heir. Children were explained to me as one explains land management or the breeding of horses—necessary to preserve the estate, preferably male, and best produced before one developed inconvenient opinions about the woman selected to bear them.” His gaze lowered briefly to the Medici signet. “I believed I disliked the idea. In truth, I disliked everything my family had built around it.”

The sunlight on the floor had reached the leg of the empty chair beside him. It washed the carved wood in gold and stopped several feet short of his shoes.

“With Elisabetta, yes.”

No ornament. No careful delay to soften it.

For a moment the dining room gave way to an older house: wisteria beyond the loggia, chalk dust on dark skirts, a black cat stretched across a page of calculations as though mathematics existed for its comfort. Elisabetta had possessed a talent for making permanence seem less like a prison and ordinary life seem vast.

“I wanted a daughter,” Filippo said. “Not fatherhood in the abstract. Not an heir. A daughter who was hers and mine.”

His eyes remained on the bright edge of the room rather than Jackie.

“I imagined her with Elisabetta’s mind and my certainty, which would have made her intolerable by the age of six. Elisabetta maintained that this was justice.” The faintest warmth entered his voice. “I argued for one child. She said three, because she had been an only child and regarded the condition as a moral failing. We disagreed about names for people who could never exist.”

The memory did not make him smile. It settled into his face more quietly than grief, worn smooth by the decades he had carried it.

“By then, I had been dead for more than fifty years. My body could preserve life indefinitely. It could heal wounds, survive injuries that should have ended me, turn another person into what I am.” His fingers rested flat on the table. “It could not begin a life.”

The contradiction had once seemed obscene. Now it was only true.

“Elisabetta knew I wanted it. I never told her plainly, but she was rarely inconvenienced by my attempts at secrecy.” His gaze returned to Jackie. “She did not ask me to apologise for the wanting. She did not offer consolation. She simply allowed it to be real.”

Above them, the Muffliato kept the bedroom utterly silent. The house appeared to be holding its breath around Theo and Draco upstairs, the warm meal below, and the future no one had yet brought to the same table.

Filippo’s attention flicked once towards the ceiling.

“Your husband was quite clear on the subject before the conversation became concerned with Frenchmen,” he said. “He may be gratified to learn that one person heard him without collapsing in laughter.”

The dry note lasted only a moment.

Then he looked at Jackie fully. Not assessing. Not prying for sport. The careful attention he gave a question only when he had decided he was prepared for its answer.

“Is that why you ask, Jacqueline?”

Theo’s eyes narrowed.

From the kitchen came the faint rattle of the extractor fan. Steam clouded the open cartons and softened the hard afternoon light. Draco corrected his shirt as he moved towards the empty place setting, fingers discovering the mismatched buttons one by one. Colour rose into his ears. He began again from the bottom without comment.

Filippo looked back to Jackie.

“Curiosity is not conspiracy, cara. Nor will I mistake overhearing a private conversation for an invitation to enter it.”

The room quieted.

Theo’s gaze moved from Jackie to Draco. He had heard enough upstairs—Draco’s mistaken answer, the careful distinctions, the future spoken before anyone realised which question was being discussed. For once, he did not use the silence to force either of them into it.

“Tuesday,” he said.

Draco’s hands stopped on the final button.

Theo rested his head against the chair and closed his eyes. The climb had left a faint strain around his mouth, though his voice remained steady.

“Not tomorrow. Tomorrow I have enough people deciding what I am.” His thumb moved over the edge of his wedding band. “Tuesday, we decide what comes next.”

Filippo watched him for a moment. No correction. No reminder that the formal decision belonged partly to his sire and partly to Viola. This was not that decision, and Theo had chosen the distinction precisely.

Draco took the chair beside him. Before reaching for his plate, he set one hand palm-up on the narrow space between their seats. Theo opened his eyes, looked at it, and placed his cold hand over Draco’s warm one.

Then he glanced towards Jackie.

“And I am not a cat.”

Filippo reached for the nearest unopened carton and moved it within Draco’s reach.

“A cat,” he said, “would have remained downstairs.”

The routine belonged to Haven House now: the Sable Room, the shaded lamps, clean linen, water waiting on the bedside table.

Theo fed without incident.

Filippo remained close enough to intervene and still enough that he never had to. Draco waited beyond the half-closed door. Within minutes, some of the strain left Theo’s face. Colour didn’t return—nothing so human—but the rigid economy of every movement eased. The Malice settled with him, appetite quieted to a low presence beneath the skin.

The donor left safely, well-paid and steady on their feet. The wards folded shut behind them.

Afterward, the house diminished by degrees. The cartons disappeared into the kitchen; cutlery ceased to touch porcelain; doors closed softly along the upper floors. Draco took Theo upstairs at the same careful pace he’d brought him down, though Theo leaned on him less heavily now. Luna followed, tail raised, and stationed herself at the bedroom threshold as if she’d been appointed to the night watch.

Filippo chose the library. He drew the curtains himself, inspected each seam against the coming morning, and settled beneath the green-shaded lamp with a book he did not need to finish before dawn.

Nothing else required deciding.

Sunday ended with the wards intact, the Malice fed, and every person beneath Haven House’s roof exactly where they had chosen to remain.
