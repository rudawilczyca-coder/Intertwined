### Sable

“I want to stay,” Theo said. “If you’re asking whether I’ve sat them down and offered to move to Italy—no.”

“Then let us establish what you would be refusing.”

“I remember Florence.”

“You remember being unable to leave it.”

“I remember more than that.”

Filippo inclined his head. “Good. And this time, you would not be returning alone.”

Theo drew his thumb beneath the loose cuff, lifting it away from his wrist. The skin there still disliked being touched. Draco’s shirt smelled faintly of laundry soap; closer to the collar, beneath that, it still smelled of Draco. He let the cuff fall.

*All three of us.*

That was harder to dismiss than being sent back. He could dislike the argument without pretending they were the same thing.

He left the window. Luna’s ears turned after him, though the rest of her remained stretched across the sill. Theo pulled out a chair beside the table and sat, taking his time. He disliked how much better that felt.

“Go on.”

“There are people I could approach at the Italian Ministry. For Jacqueline, and for Draco. Your own circumstances would require rather more consideration.”

“Being dead does complicate a job application.”

“Yes. I suggest we refrain from leading with it.”

Theo’s mouth moved despite himself. Filippo let the brief amusement pass without trying to keep it.

“Draco has a position,” Theo said.

“He does. Would you object to his being offered another?”

“Depends on the offer.”

“Quite.”

The closed book lay between them, its pages uneven beneath the cover. Theo ran a fingertip along the seam in the table.

A different office. Different people. Draco introducing himself without having to watch what happened to their faces when he reached his surname. Theo tried to imagine it and immediately wanted it for him, which was irritating. Filippo hadn’t even needed to describe it.

“They know who the Malfoys are in Italy.”

“Some of them. They also know who I am.”

“So he gets in because you ask.”

“He gets a conversation because I ask. I cannot attend an interview on his behalf. I suspect he would object.”

“He’d be unbearable.”

“He might also be rather good at it.”

Theo looked up.

Filippo’s expression hadn’t changed. No indulgent smile, no suggestion that Draco’s competence was a charming discovery. He’d made the offer seriously.

That mattered.

It also made Theo want to pick a hole in it.

“And when somebody decides they don’t like your name either?”

“Then we shall have to endure the discovery that Italy contains disagreeable people.”

“I’m serious.”

“So am I.” Filippo rested his hand beside the book. “I can make introductions. I can find out what is available. I cannot promise that nobody will ever slight him again.”

Outside, a vehicle rattled past. Theo heard a loose part knock beneath it, then the sound thinned along the street. He kept his eyes on Filippo’s hand. The signet. The unmarked wrist.

“They knew who you were here.”

Filippo went still.

Theo hadn’t raised his voice. He hadn’t needed to. His own wrist rested against the table’s edge, where the bruising showed below the borrowed sleeve.

“Yes,” Filippo said. “I relied on that too much.”

The answer left Theo with the next words still behind his teeth.

He’d wanted an admission. Now he had one, and his wrists hurt just as much.

“In Florence,” Filippo continued, “I would be present. I know the court. I know where my influence ends.”

“You can’t stand beside me every night.”

“No. But I can do considerably more in my own city than I managed in hers.”

Theo leaned back. The chair creaked beneath him, absurdly loud in the pause.

He could hear the protection in it. There would be work to consider, and Filippo close enough to help without first negotiating across somebody else’s territory. Theo could even remember Florence without resenting every stone in it.

None of that made him want to leave.

“I like London,” he said.

“I had gathered.”

“No. Not just this house.”

Filippo waited.

“I go out at night. Walk. Sometimes for hours.” Theo glanced towards the window, then back. “I like having somewhere to go that nobody’s arranged for me.”

He stopped there. Explaining every street and every unremarkable hour would spoil it. He wanted the city outside and this house to return to. That want had been easy to hold before Filippo put Draco’s future beside it.

*And I still want it.*

He wasn’t going to pretend otherwise to make himself look generous.

“I don’t want them turning down better lives because I won’t consider leaving.”

“Then consider it.”

“I am.”

Filippo’s gaze held his for a moment. Then he inclined his head again, smaller this time.

Theo eased his wrist off the edge.

“Make enquiries,” he said. “Find out what’s actually there. Not what might be possible if everyone owes you enough favours.”

“Enquiries only?”

“Yes. No arrangements. No promises on their behalf.”

“Very well.”

“And I still want to stay.”

“I heard you the first time.”

Theo gave him a narrow look, then drew his chair a little closer to the table.

“Start with Draco. Who would you speak to?”

“Lorenzo Vannini,” Filippo said. “International Magical Co-operation. Beatrice Contarini, in treaty law. Either would be worth speaking to.”

“Are they yours?”

Filippo looked at him. “Ministry officials are rarely included among my possessions.”

“Paid by you, then.”

“No.” He settled his hand beside the book. “They know me. They respect my judgement. They are reasonable people, not my retainers.”

“Reasonable enough to refuse you?”

“Beatrice occasionally appears to enjoy it.”

Theo’s mouth twitched. That sounded more promising than another assurance of influence. He could imagine Draco tolerating an introduction; discovering that his new superior took instructions from Filippo would be another matter.

“Give him the names. Let him look into them.”

“Of course. With Jacqueline, the difficulty is rather different.”

Theo waited. From the window came a soft scrape as Luna stretched, one claw catching briefly in the wood.

“She is an outstanding Auror,” Filippo continued. “But in London she will always be Potter’s protégée. She could run the department and there would still be people congratulating him on how well he trained her.”

“He’s helped her.”

“I’m aware. His help is not the difficulty. His fame is.” Filippo paused. “Her successes will reflect very well on him. Her failures, I suspect, will belong entirely to Jacqueline Nott-Malfoy.”

Theo’s fingers flattened against the table.

“Two names,” Filippo said, “which will do her few favours when a promotion requires somebody to defend appointing her.”

“She could have been Jacqueline Malfoy,” Filippo said. “Hardly an unencumbered name. But one would have been easier to carry than two.”

“She chose to keep mine.”

“I know.”

“When she married Draco. She could have dropped it. I wouldn’t have asked her to keep it.”

Theo’s fingers pressed against the table. On paper, she had been a widow. There had been nothing requiring her to carry Nott into another marriage.

“I’d have given the bloody name up myself.”

Filippo watched him for a moment.

“I’m not suggesting you insisted, Theodore.”

“Then don’t talk about it as though it needs correcting.”

“Her choice does not. The people who will hold it against her are another matter.”

“You think they’ll forget Malfoy?”

“No. Nor would I advise Draco to pretend his history began at the Italian border. But they did not live your war. They may be more interested in what either of them can do now.”

Theo glanced down. His cuff had slipped back; he drew it over the bruising without thinking, then stopped when the fabric dragged.

He wanted to argue that Jackie would make them recognise her. Of course she would. She was good enough.

But that wasn’t much of an answer to why she should have to keep doing it.

Filippo let the silence stand before continuing.

“There are people who would value her judgement, not merely her usefulness in a dangerous room. In time, she could train others. Lead them. She need not spend her career being the exceptional young woman Potter took a chance on.” His voice softened a little. “I would very much like to see what she could make of that.”

*He wants her close.*

Theo’s thumb stopped against the cuff.

The Malice had been quiet enough to disregard all morning. He spoke quietly now, too.

*He wanted her close before we were dead. Ask him why.*

Across the table, Filippo’s hand remained beside the book. Theo looked at the plain band on his right hand.

He remembered Florence before the sickrooms and the feeding lessons. The ring. The wager. Filippo refusing Jackie’s desperate offer—not because the contract offended him, but because desperation spoiled it.

There had been a great deal since then. Enough that Theo no longer looked at that hand and thought first of what its owner could bind.

Now he did.

He shifted his wrist beneath the table. The movement hurt, briefly and usefully.

Filippo had come for him. He was here, in their house, making enquiries he had no obligation to make. Theo knew that.

He also knew how easily concern could give a man a respectable reason to ask for what he wanted.

“Would you object to my finding out?” Filippo asked.

“No.”

The answer was flat enough that Filippo waited.

“Make the enquiry.”

“Then—”

“But don’t leave yourself out.”

Filippo’s gaze settled fully on him. “Out of what?”

Theo leaned back slowly. The faint scent of expensive tobacco reached him as Filippo moved his hand; he’d grown used to it in Florence. He hadn’t missed it when he came home.

“You’ve told me why it would be good for her.”

“It would.”

“I heard you.”

He held Filippo’s eyes. The next question came more quietly.

“How much of this is her career—and how much is you wanting her close?”

Filippo did not answer at once.

Nor did he look away—not immediately. He held Theo’s gaze for the length of a breath he had no need to take. Then his eyes moved to the window, to Luna, to the false sunlight lying across the sill, and stayed there.

Theo had spent six months learning that face. He knew what it meant when Filippo looked at someone too long. He had rarely seen him decline to look at all.

“Not none,” Filippo said.

Behind Theo’s ribs, the Malice settled, satisfied.

“You would not have believed me had I said none.” Filippo’s hand left the book. He took out his pocket watch and held it closed in his palm, thumb resting on the lid. “And you would have been right not to.”

“How much?”

“I am not certain the question has a measure.” A pause. “I care for her. That will not surprise you. She made her choice a year ago, and I have never asked her to unmake it.”

“You’re asking now.”

“I am asking *you*.”

Theo’s jaw tightened. That was worse, somehow. Cleaner.

Filippo turned the watch over once.

“I will tell you the part I am least proud of.” The polish had gone out of his voice—the finish he wore for courts and arguments. What remained was slower. Plainer. “Your control was proven months ago. I ought to have presented you to London then, on my terms, instead of waiting until the terms were hers.”

Theo said nothing.

“I told myself I was giving the three of you time to choose without a court breathing on the decision. That was true.” His thumb stilled on the lid. “It was also true that I hoped you would choose Florence. All three of you. That she would be somewhere I could reach in an evening.”

In the kitchen, the clock advanced another minute.

Theo looked at his own wrist, at the bruising half-hidden by Draco’s cuff.

*He waited,* the Malice said. *We bled for it.*

Not loudly. He didn’t need to.

Theo laid his hand flat on the table and kept it there.

“So when I ask you to consider Florence,” Filippo went on, “you are entitled to wonder what else I am asking for. In your place, I would.” He set the watch beside the book, lid still closed. “But Beatrice Contarini does not appoint anyone because I enjoy their company at dinner. The positions are real. What Jacqueline could build there is real. My wanting her near does not make the rest of it a lie.”

“It makes it convenient.”

“Yes.”

No defence followed. Filippo let the word stand and did nothing to soften it.

Theo had been ready for an argument. Had half wanted one, because he could have won it. Instead he had a man telling him the unflattering part first and then waiting to be judged for it.

He disliked how much easier that made it to believe the rest.

“I would not live in your house,” Filippo said, more quietly. “I would not need to. The same city would be—” He stopped. Whatever came next, he didn’t permit it. “Sufficient.”

Luna rose, turned once on the windowsill, and lay down facing the other way.

Filippo watched her rather than Theo. When he spoke again, he did it carefully, like a man setting something heavy on a narrow shelf.

“Now tell me something true in return, Theodore. It seems only fair.” His eyes came back to Theo’s. “If Jacqueline and Draco wanted Florence—if they sat you down tonight and told you so—would you go with them?”

A beat.

“Or would you ask them to stay for you?”

Theo didn’t answer at once.

The question had gone in cleanly, the way the good ones did, with no edge to brace against. He looked at the watch lying shut beside the book, then past it, through the doorway to the hall. Two empty hooks.

“I’d go,” he said.

Filippo didn’t move.

“If they wanted it. If they sat me down tonight and said Florence, I’d pack.” The picture came too easily, and Theo didn’t care for how easily. “You know I would.”

“I do.”

“So, no. I wouldn’t ask them to stay for me.” His thumb found the platinum band and turned it once. “That’s not the problem.”

“Then tell me what is.”

Theo looked around the dining room as though it might help. It was a room. Table, chairs, the false morning lying across the floor. Windows charmed against the sun before he’d ever stood in front of one of them.

“We’ve had this house less than a year,” he said. “I’ve lived in it since January.”

“I am aware.”

“They had the windows done while I was still in your city, learning not to kill anyone. My father’s house was a house. Draco had the Dark Lord at his dinner table. This—”

He stopped. Started again, lower.

“This is the first place that’s felt like home. To any of us. Ever.” His jaw worked. “It’s not London. London can go hang. It’s *here*.”

Filippo said nothing. The silence wasn’t one of his tactical ones. Theo knew the difference by now.

“So yes. I’d go. And I’d be asking them to walk out of the only home we’ve had, before we’ve had a whole year of it, for better prospects.” He lifted one shoulder, carefully. “Maybe that’s the right trade. I’m not going to be the one who makes it for them.”

“And if they choose to make it?”

“Then I’ll go. And complain about your sun for a century.”

The corner of Filippo’s mouth moved. It didn’t last.

“I have lived in one city for a very long time, Theodore,” he said. “I understand the argument better than you might suppose.” He touched the watch but didn’t pick it up. “I shall write to Beatrice regardless. And to Lorenzo. What they do with the answers is theirs.”

“Fine.”

Theo held his gaze a moment longer, then let his eyes drop to the watch.

“And the other thing.”

Filippo waited.

“You waited for her.” It wasn’t a question. “I paid for it.”

“Yes.”

*Three days,* the Malice said, very softly. *Ask for three of his.*

*Pax.*

He went. Slowly, the way He always did, like a dog lying down under protest.

“Don’t do it again,” Theo said. “Whatever you want. From her, from any of us. Don’t wait on my life to get it.”

Filippo was quiet long enough for the kitchen clock to fill the gap.

“On Wednesday,” he said at last, “I can make that very simple. Once I release you, I have nothing to wait on.” His eyes went to the window. “And nothing to stand behind when Viola sends for you. She will not need to ask me first.”

“Then she’ll have to ask me.”

Filippo looked at him properly, for once, without the assessing patience he kept for strangers. Then he looked away again, as if that had cost more than he’d budgeted for.

“She will not enjoy that.”

“She doesn’t have to.”

Luna dropped from the sill with a soft thump, crossed the room, and sat squarely in the sunlight at Theo’s feet, as if the matter were settled.

Filippo picked up his book. Opened it. Same page.

Theo let him have it.
