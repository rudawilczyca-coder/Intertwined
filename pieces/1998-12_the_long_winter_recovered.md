**The Long Winter**
*December 1998 – January 1999 | The Forbidden Forest's edge*

---

The cold at the treeline had a discipline to it. Draco had come to respect that.

It arrived the same way every morning — first at the seams of his gloves, then the hinge of his jaw, then deep in the bones of his feet, no matter what warming charms he layered into his boots. By the second week of December he'd stopped fighting it. The cold was honest. It made no exceptions for his name, asked nothing of him, lied to him about nothing. There was so little left in his life he could say that about.

He stood where the lawn surrendered to the trees, in the blue hour before the castle properly woke, and he waited.

Jackie's scarf was wound twice around his fist beneath his cloak. Bronze and blue — colors he'd been raised to sneer at, now worn against his skin like a penance. He'd told himself, the first morning, that he was holding it for her. By the tenth morning he'd stopped pretending the scarf was for anyone but himself.

The forest gave him nothing. It never did. Black trunks, white snow, the occasional shudder of a branch unloading its burden. Once, in the second week, a flash of movement deep between the trees had stopped his heart entirely — and it had been a thestral, picking its skeletal way through the drifts, regarding him with the bland pity of a creature that only the haunted could see.

*Run fast, Jackie,* he'd told her, while her golden eyes still held something of the girl inside them. *But come back to me.*

He checked his watch. Seven minutes left of the hour. He never gave the forest more than the hour — that was the bargain he'd struck with himself, the leash he kept on his own madness. An hour was devotion. More than that was unraveling, and Malfoys did not unravel where anyone might see.

The snow began again, fine as ash. Draco turned his collar up and watched the treeline until his time was spent.

*Tomorrow, then.*

He walked back to the castle without looking over his shoulder. That was the hardest discipline of all.

---

Theo watched him come in.

He'd claimed the armchair nearest the common room entrance for precisely this purpose, though he'd die before admitting it — sprawled with a book he wasn't reading, one leg hooked over the armrest, the picture of a boy with nowhere to be. The portrait door ground open at half eight, same as every morning, and Draco came through it carrying the cold on him like a second cloak. Red-rimmed eyes. White past pale. Snow melting in his hair because he'd been too proud to charm it away.

*You absolute martyr,* Theo thought, and turned a page he hadn't read.

"Coursework again?" he asked, not looking up.

"Arithmancy." Draco's voice came out rusted. He cleared it. "Vector's set a brutal revision schedule."

"Mm. Strange how Arithmancy lives at the bottom of the grounds these days." Theo let the words land soft, no barb on them — or only a small one, sheathed. Draco's glare could have stripped paint. Theo met it with the mild, unbothered blink he'd spent seventeen years perfecting, and after a moment Draco dropped into the chair across from him, defeated by exhaustion more than by wit.

This was the truce. Neither of them had negotiated it; it had simply condensed around Jackie's absence like frost on glass. With her gone — *Switzerland,* the official fiction went, *a specialist clinic, her father sends his regrets* — there was nothing left to circle each other about, and so they'd slumped back into older shapes. Mocking first-years' wandwork from the sofa. Trading sections of the Prophet. Sharing the dragonash cigarettes Theo smuggled in via a third-year with flexible ethics, smoke curling green-grey toward the lake-light in the windows while neither of them said her name.

Theo produced the cigarette case now and flicked it open one-handed. An offering.

Draco took one. Their fingers didn't touch, because Theo made sure of it. He was always making sure of it, lately — managing the distance, metering it, the way you'd handle something that could go off. Four years of practice at this. He was very good at it. He watched Draco bend to the lighter flame, lashes white-blond against his cheek, and filed the image away in the locked drawer where he kept everything he refused to want, and told himself, as he always did, that the drawer was deep enough.

"You'll be at your mother's ball," Theo said. Not a question.

"Obligation." Smoke leaked through Draco's teeth. "You?"

"My father's accepted on behalf of both Notts." Theo's smile was a thin, bladed thing. "Apparently there's to be an announcement worth toasting."

He watched that one land. Watched Draco's hand still, the cigarette burning forgotten between his fingers. The betrothal — formalized in September, papered over in contracts and vault transfers — was to be made public at the Malfoys' own New Year's Eve ball. Jackie Taylor's engagement to Theodore Nott, announced beneath Narcissa's chandeliers, while the bride ran on four legs through the dark of the Forbidden Forest.

There was a version of Theo that relished this. He could feel that version under his sternum, oily and warm, whispering that the look on Draco's face was *winning.* He was less sure, lately, that he wanted what that voice wanted. He was less sure the voice was getting quieter, either.

"It's grotesque," Draco said finally, flat as slate. "Announcing it while she's not even—" He caught himself. Rebuilt his face. "While she's abroad."

"Yes," Theo agreed pleasantly. "Grotesque. The orchestra's meant to be excellent, though."

Draco looked at him with naked loathing, and Theo absorbed it the way he absorbed everything Draco aimed at him — gratefully, secretly, because even hatred was attention, and he had learned at fourteen to live on scraps.

---

The ball was worse than grotesque. It was *boring,* on top of everything, which Draco hadn't anticipated and somehow couldn't forgive.

He stood beneath his mother's chandeliers in dress robes that fit perfectly and a smile that didn't, and he watched Cyrus Taylor raise a glass of champagne the color of weak sunlight. The man had a public voice like warm oil. *Delighted... two great houses... my daughter's profound regret that her health keeps her abroad... to the happy couple.*

The room drank. Draco drank. The crystal sang faintly against his teeth and he thought: *she is in the forest right now. It is nine below zero. She is sleeping in a pile of animals that could tear her apart, because her own body is a trap her family sold instead of fixing, and you are toasting it, you are all toasting it—*

"Smile, darling," his mother murmured at his shoulder, not unkindly. "People are looking."

People were always looking. He smiled.

Across the ballroom, Theodore Nott stood beside his father in robes a season out of fashion, accepting congratulations for an engagement to a girl who wasn't there, and for one bare instant his eyes met Draco's over the crowd. Neither of them arranged their face in time. What passed between them wasn't friendship and it wasn't rivalry. It was two boys standing in the wreckage of the same lie, recognizing each other.

Then Theo lifted his glass a fraction — *to the happy couple* — with such precise, vicious irony that Draco nearly laughed into his champagne.

He went back to school the next morning. He told his mother it was coursework.

---

The thaw came early, the first week of January. Draco heard it before he saw it — the grounds dripping, the snow gone rotten and grey, the gutters of the castle running like spring. He walked his hour that morning with his heart behaving strangely, jumping at every sound the melting forest made, and he'd nearly talked himself out of hope entirely when the alpha stepped out of the trees.

It was enormous. Grey-hackled, scar-lipped, and it came forward stiff-legged with its head low and a growl rolling out of it like distant stonework collapsing. Draco's wand was in his hand before he'd decided anything. *Don't,* he told himself, *don't, if you curse it she'll never—*

A second wolf hit the space between them.

Ruddy-brown, ribs showing through winter coat, and it planted itself in front of Draco with its hackles raised at the alpha — at its own pack leader — and held there. The growl that came out of it was thinner than the alpha's, frayed at the edges, but it didn't waver. The two animals stood locked for an unbearable length of time, breath steaming, the whole forest holding still around them.

The alpha looked away first. Lowered its head, swung around, retreated into the dark of the trees. From somewhere deep in the forest the pack lifted a howl — long, layered, mournful as church bells — and the brown wolf threw back its head and answered with one last note, and the sound had so much grief in it that Draco's eyes stung before he understood why.

Then the wolf turned to look at him with golden eyes, and the air folded, and the animal was gone.

Jackie knelt in the slush where it had been.

Bare, steaming, shaking so violently it looked like a seizure — and so thin, Merlin, so *reduced,* her shoulder blades like folded wings, a month of winter carved out of her. Draco's cloak was off his shoulders and around her before his next heartbeat. He went down on his knees in the freezing melt without registering it.

"You waited..." Her voice was a ruin. A month without words. She got out that much and her eyes rolled back and she pitched forward into him.

He caught her. Of course he caught her.

"Every day," he told her, though she couldn't hear it. His own voice didn't sound like his. He wrapped the cloak tighter, worked her scarf — *her* scarf, carried against his heart for thirty-one mornings — around her neck with hands that wouldn't fully obey him. She weighed nothing. She weighed less than the fear he'd been carrying.

The hospital wing was closest, and Pomfrey was competent, and Pomfrey would have an owl in the air to Cyrus Taylor within the hour. The thought rose and he discarded it between one stride and the next.

Draco gathered her up and carried her toward the dungeons instead — through the side courtyard, down the servants' stair, every back way he'd memorized in six years of needing not to be seen. Her breath came shallow against his collar. Once, half-surfacing, she made a sound that wasn't quite a word, and the grip of her hand twisted weakly into his shirt-front and held.

"I've got you," he said quietly, to the empty corridor, to the cold stone, to whatever was listening. "You came back. I've got you."

He didn't pass a soul the whole way down. The castle, just this once, let him keep something.
